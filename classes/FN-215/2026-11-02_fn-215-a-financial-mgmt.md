# Bond Characteristics

- Bonds issued by governments or large corporations for long-term capital needs
- Key features of a bond (illustrated with a 10-year “Shu” bond, 3% coupon):
  - Coupon payment: coupon rate x face value (default face value = $1,000, so $30/year)
  - Par/face value: $1,000, returned at maturity
  - Maturity date: date face value is returned (e.g., Nov 2, 2036 for a bond issued today)
- Call provision: issuer can redeem bonds early, paying face value + premium (e.g., 6%)
  - Issuers call back when market interest rate drops below coupon rate (cheaper to refinance)
  - Favors issuers, so callable bonds carry higher coupon rates
- Sinking fund: reserved account issuers must maintain to guarantee coupon and face value payments
  - Failure to maintain = bankruptcy filing; strict regulatory requirement
- Convertible bonds: investors can convert 1 bond to a set number of shares (e.g., 3 shares)
  - Investors convert when stock price is high, then sell immediately
  - Favors investors, so convertible bonds carry lower coupon rates

# Bond Types

- Treasury bonds: issued by the Federal Reserve, zero default risk, but high interest rate sensitivity
- Corporate bonds: exposed to default risk; higher risk = higher coupon rate
- Municipal bonds: issued by state/local governments, coupons and face value tax-exempt for local residents
  - Tax benefit favors investors, so coupon rates are lower than comparable corporate bonds
- Foreign bonds: carry default risk and foreign exchange rate risk

# Bond Pricing and Interest Rate Relationships

- Bond price calculated using Excel PV function:
  - Rate = market interest rate (not coupon rate)
  - PMT = coupon rate x face value
  - FV = face value ($1,000)
  - NPER = number of years (or periods)
- Key pricing patterns (3% coupon, 10-year bond):
  - Discount rate 5%: price = $845.57 (discount bond, below par)
  - Discount rate 3%: price = $1,000 (par bond)
  - Discount rate 2%: price above $1,000 (premium bond)
- Inverse relationship: interest rate up = bond price down, and vice versa
  - Economic logic: coupon rate is the bond’s return; market interest rate is the opportunity cost
- Three bond price types:
  - Discount bond: coupon rate < interest rate, price rises toward $1,000 at maturity
  - Par bond: coupon rate = interest rate, price stays at $1,000
  - Premium bond: coupon rate > interest rate, price falls toward $1,000 at maturity
- Semiannual coupon adjustment: divide rate and PMT by 2, multiply NPER by 2
  - Example: 3% coupon, 5% rate, semiannual = $844.11
- Silicon Valley Bank cited as real-world example: bond-heavy portfolio collapsed when Fed raised rates
