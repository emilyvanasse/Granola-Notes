# Shareholder Types and Voting Rights

- Common stockholders: 1 vote per share, uncertain dividends
- Preferred stockholders: no voting rights, fixed/regular dividends (priced as a perpetuity: PMT / r)
- Classified common stock (Class A, B, etc.): same voting rights, different voting power per share
  - Google example: Class A (public, 1 vote/share), Class B (founders/executives, 10 votes/share)
  - Purpose: lets founders maintain control without holding a majority of shares
- Majority shareholders: hold >50% of shares; blockholder threshold is >5%

# Shareholder Rights and Takeover Defenses

- Proxy voting: shareholders can transfer voting rights to another party (e.g., a blockholder) to vote on their behalf
  - Activist investors (hedge funds, asset managers) collect proxies to push for board changes or CEO compensation redesign
- Preemptive rights: existing shareholders can buy newly issued shares pro rata before others, to maintain their ownership percentage
- Three anti-takeover defenses:
  1. Staggered board: limit board replacement to no more than 1/3 per year
  2. Supermajority threshold: raise acquisition approval from 50% to 75% of shareholders
  3. Poison pill: issue new shares at a deep discount to existing shareholders, making acquisition prohibitively expensive
     - Classic example: Gucci vs. Louis Vuitton (late 1990s), Gucci issued shares at \~$2 to its own shareholders vs. \~$50 for LV

# Stock Valuation: Dividend Discount and Gordon Growth Model

- Two models for pricing common stock:
  - Dividend Discount Model (DDM): discount each projected future dividend back to present value, then sum
  - Enterprise Valuation Model: uses free cash flow to value the firm, then derive per-share price
- Three-step pricing framework (applies to all securities):
  1. Identify future cash flows
  2. Determine the discount rate
  3. Discount each cash flow to time 0 and sum
- Gordon Growth Model (GGM): special case of DDM assuming constant dividend growth rate (g)
  - Formula: P = D1 / (Rs - g)
  - D1 = next year’s expected dividend; Rs = required rate of return; g = constant dividend growth rate
  - If only D0 is given: D1 = D0 × (1 + g), then apply the same formula
- Key Excel reminder: use parentheses around (Rs - g) in the denominator to avoid order-of-operations errors
- Example results worked through in class:
  - Ex. 1: P = $2 / (7.5% - 5.5%) = $100
  - Ex. 2: g = 6.5% - (3/86) × 100 ≈ 3.01%
  - Ex. 3: Rs = (2/95) + 5.5% ≈ 7.61%
