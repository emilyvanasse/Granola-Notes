## Summary

# Blind Box Economics and Consumer Psychology

- Pop Mart blind boxes: $20/unit, $40B annual global sales; 44+ stores including 2 in Connecticut (Norwalk, Danbury Mall)
- Core appeal: gambling psychology, risk/return tradeoff
  - Rare "secret" figure: 1-in-72 chance, resale value ~$300
  - Expected value calculation: $300 × (1/72) ≈ $4.17, so rational spend is under ~$5 per box
  - At $20/box, buying 72 boxes costs ~$1,440 for a $300 payoff: negative expected return
- Secondary investment angle: buyers hold unopened boxes anticipating scarcity-driven resale appreciation (parallel to Pokémon cards)
- Lipstick Effect: luxury spending falls in downturns, but small indulgences rise
  - Estée Lauder data: lipstick sales peak when GDP bottoms
  - Blind boxes follow same pattern: affordable treat when cost of living is high

# Stock Market Structure: NYSE vs. NASDAQ

- Stock markets are secondary markets (trading between investors, not directly with issuing companies)
- Two dominant exchanges: NYSE (physical location, auction-based) and NASDAQ (dealer-based, OTC)
- Broker vs. dealer distinction:
  - Brokers: match buyers and sellers, do not hold inventory, earn commission; lower capital requirement
  - Dealers: buy securities directly from sellers, hold inventory, resell at higher price; face inventory risk
  - Key test: does the middleman trade directly with both sides? Yes = dealer; No = broker
- Bid-ask spread = ask price minus bid price (not bid minus ask)
  - Narrower spread = more liquid market (e.g., Microsoft)
  - Spread rarely negative: dealers profit by buying low and selling high
- FINRA (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority) regulates brokers and dealers; operates under SEC oversight
- Broker market analogy: dating app matches parties without becoming one of them; LinkedIn cited as surprisingly high success rate for finance professionals

# Market Indexes

- Three major benchmarks:
  - Dow Jones Industrial Average: tracks 30 largest companies, price-weighted (high-price stocks have outsized impact); currently ~52,000
  - S&P 500: tracks 500 companies, market-cap weighted; currently ~5,758; most commonly used benchmark due to diversity
  - NASDAQ Composite: tracks 3,000+ firms, market-cap weighted; heavily skewed toward tech, higher volatility
- Price-weighted vs. market-cap weighted:
  - Price-weighted: a 1% move in a $500 stock has 10× the index impact of a 1% move in a $50 stock
  - Market-cap weighted: impact determined by price × shares outstanding, so a low-price/high-volume company can equal a high-price/low-volume one

# Market Efficiency and Financial Statements

- Efficient market: stock price fully reflects intrinsic value when supply = demand and transaction costs = zero
  - Not all stocks equally efficient: large, profitable, low-leverage, transparent firms attract higher demand and trade more efficiently
  - Arbitrage (buy low, sell high) only exists in inefficient markets where price gaps persist due to information asymmetry
- 10-K annual report: 4 sections
  - Section 1: business operations, new products, market entries
  - Section 2: financial data (EPS, profit margin)
  - Section 3: leadership, board of directors, compensation
  - Section 4: supplemental documents
- Four financial statements covered in Chapter 3:
  - Balance sheet: assets = liabilities + shareholders' equity; snapshot of what company owns and owes
  - Income statement: revenue, costs, profit over a period; most closely watched by investors
  - Statement of cash flows: beginning and ending cash balances, activity breakdown
  - Statement of shareholders' equity: equity held by stockholders
