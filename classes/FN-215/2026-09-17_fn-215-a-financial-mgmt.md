## Summary

# Types of Financial Institutions

- Investment banks: serve large corporations
  - Help raise capital (e.g., IPOs) or facilitate M&A
  - Examples: Goldman Sachs
- Commercial banks: serve everyday people
  - Collect deposits at one rate, lend at a higher rate
  - Gross spread = difference between deposit rate and lending rate (not yet profit; costs must be deducted)
  - Big 4 (all financial services corporations): JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, BofA, Citi
- Credit unions: similar to commercial banks but membership-restricted
  - Members share a common bond (e.g., Boeing, Sikorsky employees)
  - Not-for-profit; borrowing costs lower than banks
  - Largest example: Navy Federal Credit Union
- Financial services corporations: large conglomerates combining banking, wealth management, real estate, etc.

# Banking Regulation and FDIC

- FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation): primary regulator for most US banks
  - Insures eligible depositors up to $250,000 per account if a bank fails
  - Payout timeline can take months; diversifying across banks is advisable
- Becoming a bank: two charter paths
  - National charter (multi-state): apply to OCC (Office of the Comptroller of the Currency)
  - State charter (single state, e.g., Newton Savings Bank): apply to state banking authority
- Fintech example: Revolut (UK-based, founded early 2010s as a payments company)
  - Received OCC approval to operate as a US bank in September 2026
  - US headquarters in Stamford, CT; 160+ branches as of recently

# 10-Year Treasury Yield and Bank Profitability

- 10-year Treasury yield: benchmark rate for borrowing across the economy
  - Currently approaching 5%
  - Directly linked to mortgage rates (30-year mortgage now near 7%)
- When Treasury yield rises, cost of borrowing rises for individuals and businesses
- Banks profit from the spread between deposit rates (e.g., 0.1%) and lending rates (e.g., ~7%)

# Investment Funds

- Mutual funds and ETFs: for everyday investors
  - Allow fractional share ownership across diversified portfolios
  - Key difference: ETFs trade like stocks (anytime markets are open); mutual funds trade once daily at market close
  - Risk depends on underlying holdings, not the fund type itself
- Hedge funds: for institutions and high-net-worth individuals ($1M+ excluding primary residence)
  - Goal is high return, not risk diversification; use high leverage
  - Fee structure: management fee (up to ~4%) plus performance fee above a target threshold
  - Less regulated than ETFs/mutual funds
- Pension funds (e.g., 401k): employer-funded retirement accounts
  - Contributions deducted from each paycheck; early withdrawal incurs penalties
  - Pre-pandemic crisis: aggressive strategies left some funds unable to meet withdrawal demands
  - Now more tightly regulated toward safer strategies
- Insurance companies: collect premiums, pay claims on injury/illness
- Private equity: acquires undervalued companies, restructures, then exits via IPO or sale
  - Now dominant in M&A; owns many major retail/food chains
  - Subway case study: closed ~1/3 of US locations, profit tripled
    - Cut staff per location, switched to frozen/pre-prepared food (via Cisco)
    - Eliminated local sourcing agents to reduce costs
    - Raised prices; fewer locations created perceived scarcity

# Stock Markets and Bid-Ask Spread

- Primary vs. secondary markets
  - Primary: buying newly issued stock directly from the issuer
  - Secondary: trading existing shares between investors
- NYSE (physical location exchange) vs. NASDAQ (electronic)
  - NYSE membership historically required; seat cost $50,000+/year (5 years ago)
- Bid-ask mechanics
  - Bid price: highest price a buyer will pay
  - Ask price: lowest price a seller will accept
  - Matching order: highest bidder paired with lowest asker first
- Bid-ask spread as a liquidity measure
  - Wide spread: low liquidity, fewer traders, less price transparency (typical of smaller/less-known firms)
  - Narrow spread: high liquidity, high trading volume, strong information transparency (typical of large-cap firms)
  - Trading volume is an alternative liquidity measure
