## Summary

### Comparative Advantage and Specialization Review

- Sophia's PPF: 120 grapes or 60 wheat; Riley's: 60 grapes or 20 wheat
- Opportunity costs:
  - Sophia: 1 grape = ½ wheat; 1 wheat = 2 grapes
  - Riley: 1 grape = ⅓ wheat; 1 wheat = 3 grapes
- Absolute advantage: Sophia in both (produces more of each outright)
- Comparative advantage:
  - Riley in grapes (lower opportunity cost: ⅓ vs. ½ wheat)
  - Sophia in wheat (lower opportunity cost: 2 vs. 3 grapes)

### Gains from Specialization

- Under autarky: Sophia consumes 40/40, Riley 15/15; joint output = 55 grapes, 55 wheat
- Under full specialization: Sophia makes 0 grapes/60 wheat; Riley makes 60 grapes/0 wheat
- Joint output rises to 60 grapes and 60 wheat
  - Specialization "creates" 5 extra grapes and 5 extra wheat from nothing
- Key insight: specialization only makes sense when trade is possible
  - Without trade (e.g., Little House on the Prairie), you must produce everything yourself

### Terms of Trade

- Trade must fall within both parties' opportunity costs, or they're better off alone
- 1 wheat trades for between 2 and 3 grapes
  - Below 2: Sophia refuses (can get 2 grapes internally)
  - Above 3: Riley refuses (only costs her 3 grapes internally)
- 1 grape trades for between ⅓ and ½ wheat (same logic, reversed)
- Additional constraints on any valid trade:
  - Can't trade more than you have
  - Both parties must end up with strictly more than under autarky

### Working Through a Valid Trade

- Sophia has 60 wheat, needs to keep at least 41; can trade away up to 19 wheat
- Riley has 60 grapes, needs to keep at least 16; can trade away up to 44 grapes
- Valid trade range: 16–19 wheat for 41–44 grapes
- Example trade chosen: Sophia gives 18 wheat, Riley gives 42 grapes
  - Post-trade: Sophia has 42 grapes + 42 wheat; Riley has 18 grapes + 18 wheat
  - Both consume outside their individual PPFs: the gains from trade

### Demand Curves and Shifters

- Law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls; demand curves always slope down
- Change in price = movement *along* the curve (change in quantity demanded)
- Change in anything else = potential *shift* of the curve (change in demand)
- Demand curve equation example: P = -1/20 · QD + 10
  - Same info expressible as a graph, equation, or demand schedule (table)
- Key demand shifters:
  - Tastes/preferences (e.g., health study boosts coffee demand at same price)
  - Price of related goods
  - Income: direction of shift depends on good type
    - Normal good: income up → demand up (e.g., lattes, Taco Bell for some)
    - Inferior good: income up → demand down (e.g., instant coffee, ramen, frozen food)
  - Taxes
- "Inferior" is a technical definition, not a value judgment
