## Summary

### The Federal Reserve: Background and Functions

- Fed founded 1913, modeled on German and English central banks
- Four core functions:
  - Regulator and monitor of commercial banks (Citi, BofA, Wells Fargo)
  - Intermediary between banks for transfers
  - Bank for banks: holds reserves, moves money between accounts
  - Monopoly on currency production (all bills are Federal Reserve Notes)
- Key historical milestones:
  - 1935: Banking Act created the FOMC; centralized monetary policy from 12 regional districts
  - 1951: Fed-Treasury Accord split Fed and Congress into fully separate institutions
  - 1977: Dual mandate established by Congress (price stability + maximum employment)
  - 2008: Congress authorized interest on reserves (IORB), changing the monetary policy framework entirely

### Structure: Districts, Board, and FOMC

- 12 regional Fed districts drawn in 1913 based on equal population, not geography
  - Map now looks skewed: entire West Coast = one district (San Francisco); Northeast has many small ones
  - Each district has its own president and identity (St. Louis: data/FRED; Minneapolis: academic research; Atlanta: nowcasting; San Francisco: climate)
- Board of Governors: 7 members, based in DC
  - Appointed by the president, confirmed by the Senate
  - 14-year staggered terms to insulate from political pressure
  - In practice, most leave after a few years for lucrative private-sector roles (Wall Street, consulting, think tanks)
- FOMC: 12 voting members, decides all monetary policy
  - All 7 Board of Governors members, always
  - New York Fed president, always (implements policy via Wall Street connections)
  - 4 of the remaining 11 regional presidents, rotating annually
- The Troika: chair, vice chair, and NY Fed president; if these three agree, policy follows
  - Current chair: Kevin Warsh; vice chair: Philip Jefferson; NY president: John Williams

### Fed Independence: Political Pressures and Structural Flaws

- Independence is structural but not absolute
  - Nixon pressured Arthur Burns to “juice the economy” before reelection; LBJ did similarly
  - Recent drama: Trump attempted to fire Jerome Powell; Lisa Cook was fired; courts intervened
  - Powell refused to leave, staying on as a Board member after stepping down as chair
- Board vacancies are common: members leave early, and Congress struggles to confirm replacements
- FOMC meeting rules: if 3+ members gather, it’s an official meeting
  - Minutes released one month later (anonymized); full transcripts released after 5 years
  - Members cannot socialize as a group; one-on-ones only

### Monetary Policy: Expansionary vs. Contractionary

- Analogy: driving at exactly 80 mph on a hilly road; gas = expansionary, brake = contractionary
- Main tool: Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB), a rate the Fed sets directly (not market-determined)
- Expansionary (step on the gas):
  - Lower IORB → holding reserves less attractive → banks lend more
  - Supply of loanable funds increases → market interest rates fall
  - Investment and consumption rise → velocity growth increases → aggregate demand rises
- Contractionary (step on the brake):
  - Raise IORB → holding reserves more attractive (safest possible asset) → banks lend less
  - Supply of loanable funds falls → market interest rates rise
  - Investment and consumption fall → nominal spending falls → aggregate demand falls
- IORB framework is post-2008; pre-2008 thinking focused on money supply, not interest rates
  - Many finance professionals trained before 2008 still think in the old framework
