## Summary

### Loanable Funds Model: Equilibrium Practice

- Review problem: S = 20 + 10R, I = 100 – 10R
  - Equilibrium: set S = I, solve for R
  - Solution: quantity = 60, interest rate = 4%
- Shift scenario: save 20 less at every interest rate
  - New savings equation: (20 – 20) + 10R = 10R
  - New equilibrium: quantity = 50, interest rate = 5%
  - Key point: can’t just adjust the old equilibrium quantity; must rewrite the savings equation and re-solve

### Crowding Out

- Government enters loanable funds market as a net borrower (US borrowing \~$2T this year)
  - Demand for loanable funds shifts right: new demand = old firm demand + government borrowing
- Effects at new equilibrium:
  - Interest rate rises
  - Total savings increases (higher rates incentivize delayed consumption)
  - Total investment rises, but by less than government borrowed
- Private investment falls because higher rates reduce firm borrowing
  - Visible on graph: original demand curve (firms only) shows lower quantity at higher rate
- Crowding out: government borrowing raises rates, increases savings, but displaces private investment
  - Not a normative claim, just a positive/descriptive result

### National Savings Identity

- Derived from three ingredients:
  1. GDP: Y = C + I + G + NX
  2. Closed economy assumption: NX = 0
  3. Loanable funds equilibrium: S = I
- Derivation steps:
  - Drop NX: Y = C + I + G
  - Rearrange: Y – C – G = I, so S = Y – C – G
  - Add and subtract T (taxes): S = (Y – T – C) + (T – G)
- Components:
  - Private savings: Y – T – C (income after taxes, minus consumption)
  - Public savings: T – G (tax revenue minus government spending)
- US government is a net borrower, so public savings is negative; private savings funds investment
- Data available via Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA)
