## Summary

### What Is Economics

- Economics defined as "the study of purposive human action": an analytical social science
  - Analytical: rigorous, logical, testable; uses math, graphs, equations
  - Social science: science in the realm of people
  - Purposive: intentional actions only (not reflexes)
- Scarcity is the foundation: because resources are limited, choices must be made
  - Without scarcity, no choices; without choices, no economics
  - Everything is scarce: time, water, carbon, attention
- Economics does not require money; every intentional decision is an economic one

### Core Principles: Margins and Incentives

- Decisions are made on the margin: "one more or one less," not all-or-nothing
  - Formalized around 1870 (marginal revolution), alongside similar independent discoveries
  - Böhm-Bawerk's farmer parable: farmer cuts lowest-value use (pigeon feed) when grain is lost, not a uniform cut across all uses
  - Pre-1870: the diamond-water paradox was unsolvable without marginal thinking
- Incentives shape all actions, including unintended ones
  - Example: parents offer a car for straight A's
    - Intended incentive: study harder
    - Unintended incentive: raises the benefit of cheating when one grade is at risk
  - Second-order thinking separates economists from non-economists

### Micro vs. Macro

- Micro: individual choices, single markets, firms, or industries
  - Partial equilibrium: supply meets demand in one market
  - Example: gas prices rising and EV adoption
- Macro: aggregates all individual decisions up to an entire economy (usually country-level)
  - General equilibrium: ripple effects across all markets simultaneously
  - Key macro topics: inflation, unemployment, total economic output

### Normative vs. Positive Analysis

- Positive: testable, factual statements about what *is*
  - Economics operates entirely in positive analysis
  - Example: "Raising the minimum wage increases low-income earnings" (testable, may be false)
- Normative: opinions about what *should be*; not economics
  - Example: "Everyone should donate to charity"
  - "Should" is a red flag for normative framing
- Economics can evaluate whether a policy achieves a normative goal, but cannot validate the goal itself
- Class norm: call out normative statements from anyone, including the professor

### Production Possibility Frontier (PPF)

- PPF shows all possible combinations of two goods given fixed resources and technology
  - Simplifying assumptions: two goods, fixed technology, fixed resources, inputs perfectly substitutable
- Points on the curve: efficient (all resources used)
- Points inside the curve: inefficient (could have more of both)
- Points outside the curve: impossible
- Opportunity cost: what is given up to produce more of the other good
  - Straight-line PPF means constant opportunity cost at every point
  - Bowed-out (realistic) PPF means opportunity cost varies; requires calculus
- Computers vs. shirts example (24 labor hours; 1 computer = 12 hrs, 1 shirt = 2 hrs):
  - Max output: 2 computers or 12 shirts
  - Opportunity cost of 1 computer: 6 shirts
  - Opportunity cost of 1 shirt: 1/6 of a computer (to be continued next class)
