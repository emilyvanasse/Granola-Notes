## Summary

### Price Taker Assumption in Trade Models

- Domestic country treated as a price taker: can buy any quantity at the world price without shifting it
- US is large vs. any single economy, but small vs. all economies combined
- Chex Mix analogy: even pooling an entire class's savings can't move the market price
- Applies to soybeans: US produces a lot, but Brazil, China, Chile together dwarf US output

### Coffee Market Setup (Free Trade Baseline)

- Domestic demand: QD = 120 - 20P; domestic supply: QS = 20P; world price = $2
- Domestic equilibrium (no trade): P = $3, Q = 60
  - CS = 90, PS = 90, Total Surplus = 180
- At world price of $2: QD = 80, QS = 40, imports = 40 units
  - CS rises to 160, PS falls to 40, Total Surplus = 200
  - Consumers gain; producers lose; net gain from trade = 20
  - Gains split: transfer from producers to consumers + new triangle of gains from trade

### Tariff Analysis (50-Cent Tariff on Coffee)

- New effective price = $2.50; domestic QS rises to 50, QD falls to 70, imports shrink to 20
- Surplus outcomes:
  - CS = 122.5 (down from 160)
  - PS = 62.5 (up from 40)
  - Government revenue = $0.50 × 20 units = $10
  - Total surplus = 195 (down from 200)
- Deadweight loss = 5 total, split into two triangles:
  - Trades no longer occurring (demand side)
  - Inefficient domestic production (supply side)
- Political economy driver: producers gain $22.50, so willing to spend up to $22.40 lobbying Congress

### Sugar Market: Tariff Taken to the Extreme

- World price = 9¢/lb; US domestic price = 20¢/lb; consumption ~20 billion lbs/year
- At free trade: US domestic supply = 0, all sugar imported
- US sugar tariff = 11¢/lb, pushing effective price to 20¢, eliminating all imports
  - Zero government revenue (price just reaches domestic equilibrium)
  - Massive deadweight loss from inefficient domestic production
  - Consumers pay more but don't notice (small per-unit cost, dispersed across millions)
  - Three large sugar producers capture concentrated gains
- Classic political economy pattern: concentrated benefits, dispersed costs

### Arguments For and Against Tariffs

- Against (economic consensus):
  - Reduces total surplus and creates deadweight loss
  - "Protect jobs" argument weak: reallocating to comparative advantage raises wages and output overall
- For (two main arguments):
  - Revenue: tariffs raise some government revenue, though nowhere near enough to replace income tax
  - National security: legitimate case for producing strategically sensitive goods domestically
    - But easily abused (e.g., mohair tariff justified as military necessity)
