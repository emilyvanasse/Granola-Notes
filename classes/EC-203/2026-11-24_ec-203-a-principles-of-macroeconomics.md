## Summary

### Fiscal Policy: The Three Levers

- Congress controls three tools to shape economic activity:
  - Taxes
  - Government spending
  - Transfer payments
- Expansionary: decrease taxes, increase spending, increase transfers
- Contractionary: raise taxes, decrease spending, decrease transfers

### Multipliers

- Spending multiplier: \~0.8 (each $1 spent raises GDP by \~$0.80)
- Tax multiplier: \~1.5 (each $1 cut raises GDP by \~$1.50)
- Tax cuts appear more effective than spending for quick stimulus
- Transfer payment multiplier tied to marginal propensity to consume (MPC)
  - US MPC estimated at \~0.25 (spend 25 cents of each new dollar)
  - $100 transfer payment yields \~$33 total increase in aggregate demand
- Multipliers vary widely by context:
  - 2008 tax rebate multiplier was \~0.1 (very ineffective)
  - Tax cuts for lower incomes have higher multipliers than for top earners
  - Well-targeted spending can reach multipliers of 2-3; average is below 1
- Crowding out reduces effectiveness: government hiring away already-employed workers offsets private spending

### Limits of Fiscal Policy

- Laws must pass House, Senate, and President: slow and politically difficult
- Congresspeople are not trained economists; calibration is poor
  - Most have taken less economics than a student finishing EC-203
- COVID example: stimulus was massively overdone, driving inflation to \~9% by 2022
- Political economy problems:
  - Tax cuts are hard to reverse (constituents resist restoration)
  - Spending increases are hard to cut (military, Social Security, Medicare)
  - Transfer payments via one-off checks are easier to control than entitlement expansions
- Ricardian equivalence: corporations recognize today’s spending = future taxes, dampening effects

### Automatic Stabilizers

- Work without new legislation, fighting aggregate demand swings automatically
- Proportional income tax: as income falls, tax burden falls, preserving disposable income
- Unemployment insurance: transfers rise automatically when jobs are lost
  - Targeted at people most likely to spend, so multiplier is higher than average
- Economists prefer these: designed carefully in advance, then self-executing

### Monetary Policy Limits and the Supply Shock Problem

- Milton Friedman: monetary policy operates with “long and variable lags”
  - Fed controls interest rates but cannot directly control borrowing decisions
  - Timing of impact differs every episode; easy to overshoot or undershoot
  - 2008: too little policy, slow recovery; 2021: too much, years of elevated inflation
- Supply shock scenario (rising inflation expectations): monetary policy cannot fix it
  - Cutting rates fixes unemployment but worsens inflation
  - Raising rates fixes inflation but worsens unemployment
  - No aggregate demand tool can solve a supply-side problem; best response is to wait
- Current Fed rate: 3.75–4%; December hike likely as inflation still \~3–3.5% (above 2% target)
