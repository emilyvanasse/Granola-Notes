## Summary

### PPF Review and Equation of a Line

- Setup: 2 goods (shirts and computers), fixed technology, fixed resources, perfectly substitutable inputs
- 24 hours total; 12 hrs/computer, 2 hrs/shirt
  - Max output: 12 shirts or 2 computers
- Efficiency equation: 12C + 2S = 24, solved to S = 12 − 6C
  - Slope of −6, Y-intercept of 12
- Classifying points on the PPF:
  - On the line: efficient (e.g. 0C, 12S or 1C, 6S)
  - Inside the line: inefficient (e.g. 1C, 3S → uses only 18 of 24 hrs)
  - Outside the line: impossible (e.g. 2C, 3S → requires 30 hrs)

### Relaxing PPF Assumptions

- New technology (computer-specific): cuts production time from 12 to 6 hrs/computer
  - Shirt intercept unchanged at 12; computer intercept doubles to 4
  - Line rotates outward on the computer axis only
  - Previously impossible points (e.g. 2C, 3S) become possible or even inefficient
- New resources (e.g. doubling hours from 24 to 48):
  - Both intercepts double: shirts to 24, computers to 4
  - Entire line shifts out equally (inputs are perfectly substitutable)
  - Technology shifts one endpoint; new resources shift the whole line
- Connection to long-run macro: new technology is a key driver of economic growth over 10–50 year horizons

### Opportunity Cost Calculation Method

- Method: divide each endpoint by itself to get 1, apply same divisor to the other side
  - Cost of 1 shirt = 1/6 computer; cost of 1 computer = 6 shirts
  - The two opportunity costs are always reciprocals of each other
- Example with Sophia and Riley (grapes and wheat):
  - Sophia: 120 grapes or 60 wheat
    - 1 grape costs ½ wheat; 1 wheat costs 2 grapes
  - Riley: 60 grapes or 20 wheat
    - 1 grape costs ⅓ wheat; 1 wheat costs 3 grapes

### Absolute vs. Comparative Advantage

- Absolute advantage: simply who can produce more
  - Sophia has absolute advantage in both goods (120 > 60 grapes; 60 > 20 wheat)
- Comparative advantage: who has the lower opportunity cost per unit
  - Grapes: Sophia gives up ½ wheat; Riley gives up ⅓ wheat → Riley has comparative advantage in grapes
  - Wheat: Sophia gives up 2 grapes; Riley gives up 3 grapes → Sophia has comparative advantage in wheat
- Key rule: impossible to have comparative advantage in both goods simultaneously
- LeBron James analogy:
  - Absolute advantage in mowing lawns (athletic, strong, efficient)
  - Opportunity cost = ~$200K speaking engagement per hour → hires a neighborhood kid for $20
  - Kid has comparative advantage in lawn care; LeBron's is in basketball/speaking

### Autarky, Specialization, and Trade

- Autarky: no trade, no specialization; consume only what you produce
  - Sophia: 40 grapes, 40 wheat; Riley: 15 grapes, 15 wheat (used for cleaner math)
- Full specialization: each producer focuses entirely on their comparative advantage good
  - Sophia: 60 wheat, 0 grapes; Riley: 60 grapes, 0 wheat
- Partial specialization noted (US still makes some cars and T-shirts despite lacking comparative advantage); class uses full specialization for simplicity
- Trade requires either different production abilities or different preferences; class focuses on production differences only
- Next class: pick up from the specialized totals and explore gains from trade
