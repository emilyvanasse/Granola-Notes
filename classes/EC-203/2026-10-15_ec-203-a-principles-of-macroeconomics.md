### JOLTS Survey and Additional Metrics

- Current Population Survey (CPS) surveys 160,000 households monthly, but response rates are declining
- JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey) surveys businesses directly, more reliable now
  - Key measures: hires, quits, openings/vacancies
- High quits = healthy labor market signal: workers only quit when confident they can find another job
- Labor market tightness: V/U (vacancies over unemployed)
  - High V/U = slack market (lots of openings per person)
  - Low V/U = tight market (few openings, lots of competition)

### Types of Unemployment

- **Frictional:** short-term, caused by the time it takes to find a job
  - Frictions = LinkedIn setup, networking, resume, applications, interviews, negotiation
  - Classic example: graduating seniors job searching
  - Duration: weeks to a couple of months; always some present in the economy
- **Structural:** longer-term, two causes:
  - Rules of the game: licensing requirements, minimum wage, ban-the-box laws, background checks
    - Ban-the-box example: removing felony checkbox increased discrimination against Black men
    - Minimum wage above equilibrium creates excess labor supply
  - Makeup of the economy: industry shifts driven by technology or trade
    - Examples: steel jobs moving to China, Uber displacing taxis, ATMs replacing bank tellers, Amazon killing Books-A-Million, electricity ending lamplighter jobs
    - Called “creative destruction” (Schumpeter): new industries destroy old ones, net positive for growth but causes unemployment in affected sectors
  - Duration: months to years (retraining takes time)

### Natural Rate of Unemployment and Cyclical Unemployment

- Natural rate (U\*) = frictional + structural unemployment
- Fed’s dual mandate: 2% inflation + maximum employment, defined as unemployment at U\*
- Cyclical unemployment = actual unemployment rate minus U\*
  - Positive cyclical: recession (e.g., 2008 financial crisis, COVID)
  - Zero cyclical: economy at potential
  - Negative cyclical: boom/expansion (e.g., 2022 post-COVID surge)
- U\* estimated at roughly 3.5–5% in the US; CBO’s current NAIRU estimate: \~4.4%
- Gray bars on FRED charts = official recessions, declared by NBER committee (based in Cambridge, MA)

### Full Employment Rate of Unemployment

- Alternative measure of U\*: balances social cost of unemployment vs. cost of unfilled vacancies
  - Ideal: one vacancy per unemployed person
  - Formula: square root of (vacancies × unemployed), a geometric average
- Advantage over NAIRU: uses real-time data, not model-based estimates
  - Available monthly at the state level, not just nationally
  - Useful for policy since labor markets vary significantly by state (e.g., California vs. Connecticut)
